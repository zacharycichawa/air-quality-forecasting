## Why PM2.5 matters for health

Fine particles (PM2.5) are small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs, and the
smallest can enter the bloodstream. Long-term exposure is associated with
cardiovascular disease (heart attacks, strokes), chronic respiratory disease, lung
cancer and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Short-term exposure during smog episodes can
worsen asthma and other respiratory and heart conditions. WHO considers that there
is no level of PM2.5 exposure below which no health effects occur, which is why the
2021 guidelines are so low.

## Who is most at risk

Children, older people, pregnant women, and people with heart or lung disease
(for example asthma or COPD) are the most sensitive to air pollution.

## General advice during a smog episode

This is general public-health information, not medical advice. On days with high
PM2.5 people are commonly advised to: limit strenuous outdoor exercise, especially
sensitive groups; keep windows closed during peak hours (in Polish winter smog, the
evening and night); use an air purifier with a HEPA filter indoors where available;
and follow local air quality alerts. People with symptoms or chronic conditions should
follow their doctor's advice.

## Main sources of PM2.5 in Poland

In Poland the dominant source of winter PM2.5 is residential heating with solid fuels
(coal and wood burned in old, inefficient stoves and boilers), often called
"low-stack emissions" (niska emisja). Road traffic is an important source of NO2 and
contributes to particulate matter in cities. Weather strongly affects concentrations:
cold, calm conditions with a low boundary layer (temperature inversions) trap
pollution near the ground, which is why smog episodes happen in the heating season.

## Policy responses in Poland

- Regional anti-smog resolutions (uchwały antysmogowe) restrict the solid fuels and
  heating devices that may be used. Kraków was the first city to ban burning coal and
  wood for home heating, in force from 1 September 2019.
- The national "Clean Air" programme (Czyste Powietrze), launched in 2018, subsidises
  replacing old boilers and insulating single-family homes.

## What this project's data shows (2019–2025)

- The network-average PM2.5 fell from 19.4 µg/m³ in 2019 to 15.5 µg/m³ in 2025
  (about −20 %). That meets the current EU annual limit (25 µg/m³) but is about three
  times the WHO 2021 guideline (5 µg/m³).
- About a third of valid station-days in 2025 exceeded the WHO daily guideline for
  PM2.5 (15 µg/m³).
- October–March daily means are about twice the summer level, with an evening peak
  linked to home heating.
- Kraków fell from the 4th most polluted city (of 42 with PM2.5 data) in 2019 to 18th
  of 72 in 2025, consistent with its solid-fuel heating ban.
